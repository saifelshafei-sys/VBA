The thing nobody tells you about finding out your husband has another family is how ordinary it is.

There's no thunderclap. No anonymous letter slid under the door. It happens on a Tuesday night, next to the dryer, with a basket of warm towels balanced on my hip and a dryer sheet stuck to the sleeve of my sweater.

Daniel got home from Providence a little after nine. He does the Providence trip twice a month—three days, two nights, a client who insists on seeing his face instead of a screen. He came in the way he always does, loosening his tie with one finger, kissing the side of my head without really looking at me, saying the drive was murder. He ate the chicken I'd kept warm for him standing up at the counter. Then he went upstairs, and by ten the shower had stopped running and the bedroom light had gone out.

Twelve years of marriage. I know the sound of him falling asleep through a ceiling.

I stayed downstairs. I usually do. I like the house at night, once the dishwasher has finished its last gurgle and the street outside has gone dark. We bought this place the year we got married, a white colonial on a cul-de-sac with a maple tree in the front yard that turns the color of a fire engine every October. Daniel wanted something newer, with an open floor plan and a three-car garage. I wanted this one. I wanted the window seat in the kitchen and the narrow back staircase and the laundry room tucked behind the pantry, where nobody would ever think to look for me.

I got the house. Daniel got to tell everyone at dinner parties that he'd let me have it. That's how most things work in our marriage. I get what I want, and he gets to believe it was his idea.

His gym bag was by the back door where he'd dropped it. It always is. Daniel has never once in twelve years carried his own gym bag to the laundry room, and I have never once said anything about it, because that is the kind of wife I am. The kind who picks things up.

So I pick it up.

I unzip it on top of the dryer, and the smell hits me first: stale sweat and damp towels, the sour, familiar smell of my husband after a long day of pretending. I start pulling things out. One gray T-shirt, damp. Shorts. A pair of socks balled together so tightly I have to peel them apart with my fingernails. A protein bar wrapper. His good headphones, which he'll swear later that he couldn't find.

And then my fingers find the zippered pocket along the side, and something inside it. Something cold. Heavier than it should be. Something that isn't a key and isn't a water bottle.

It's vibrating. Silently, steadily, against my fingertips.

It's a phone.

Not his phone. His phone is upstairs on the nightstand, face down, charging next to his reading glasses, the way it has been every night since we got married. This one is smaller. Black. Cheap. The kind of phone you buy at a gas station, still with the plastic film half-peeled off the back.

I stand there holding it for a long time.

My hands are shaking. I want that on the record. Whatever happens after this—whatever anyone says about me later—I want it understood that when I found that phone, my hands were shaking so badly I almost dropped it on the tile.

I press the side button. The screen lights up. There's a lock screen, a plain blue background, a little grid of numbers.

I shouldn't know the code. I know that. But Daniel has used the same four numbers for everything since the day I met him: his bank card, the garage door, the alarm system, the luggage lock we bought for our honeymoon. Every wife knows her husband's numbers, whether she wants to or not.

The first code I try works.

The phone opens to a home screen with almost nothing on it. No games. No email. No photos of our kitchen renovation or the dog we had to put down last spring. Just the basic apps it came with, and a single green speech bubble in the corner with a red number on it.

Three unread messages.

I notice the battery icon in the corner while I'm deciding whether to touch it. A hundred percent. Completely full. It's a strange thing to notice, I know. But that's how it goes when your life is cracking down the middle. Your brain grabs onto small, stupid details, like a person falling off a cliff grabbing at roots.

I open the messages.

There's a photo above the messages, from two days ago. I almost don't open it. Then I do.

Two little girls on a porch step, maybe four and six, in matching yellow raincoats. The older one is grinning with a gap where her front tooth used to be. The younger one is squinting into the sun with her whole face scrunched up.

She has Daniel's eyes. That's the part I can't get past. Gray, and a little too far apart, so that he always looks slightly surprised. I have looked into those eyes across a dinner table four thousand times. And there they are, in the face of a child I have never seen, on a porch I have never stood on, in a town I am now certain is not Providence.

Daddy.

I had no idea. I need you to believe that. Twelve years, and I had no idea at all.

I scroll up. The messages go back months. Did you remember the dentist? Your mother called here again, I didn't pick up. Can you bring the good sunscreen, they don't sell it here. Ordinary messages. Grocery lists and school pickups. Somebody else's marriage, being lived in our gym bag.

The microwave clock across the room glows green in the dark.

I make a note of the time.

Daniel comes down the stairs so fast he nearly falls, in his boxers and one sock, his hair flat on one side. He has the expression of a man who expects to find a burglar or a fire. What he finds is his wife standing in the laundry room holding a phone out at arm's length, like it's something dead she found in the yard.

I watch his face. I want to remember it exactly.

First there's confusion. Then he sees what's in my hand, and every bit of color drains out of him from the forehead down, as if somebody pulled a plug. His mouth opens. Nothing comes out.

"Who is N?" I say.

"Claire—"

"Who is N, Daniel? Who are the girls?"

"Claire, put it down. Please. Just put it down and let me—"

"Let you what? Explain?" My voice cracks, high and ugly. "Explain Ellie's tooth? Explain how you have daughters?"

He flinches at the name. I see it land on him. Up until that second I think some part of him was still hoping I'd only read the surface of it—a flirty text, a woman from work, something survivable. Ellie's name takes that away from him.

He sits down on the step between the kitchen and the laundry room, heavily, like his legs have been cut. He puts his face in his hands.

"How did you even find it?" he says through his fingers.

"It was in your gym bag. In the side pocket. Where I was always going to find it eventually, Daniel, because I'm the one who empties your gym bag. I'm the one who empties everything."

He lifts his head and looks at me, and there's something new in his face. Not guilt. Something closer to puzzlement.

"I never keep it in the gym bag," he says slowly. "I never bring it in the house. I keep it in the—"

He stops.

"In the what?" I say.

But he's shaking his head now, tired, beaten, rubbing his eyes. "It doesn't matter. God. It doesn't matter where I kept it."

He's right. It doesn't. Not anymore.

"How long?" I ask.

He tells me. Seven years. Her name is Nora. She lives forty minutes away, in a gray house with a porch, in a town I have driven through on my way to the outlet mall. He met her at a conference in Hartford when we were going through the worst of the fertility treatments, when I cried every month and he said he'd never needed anything but me. The girls are Ellie and June. Ellie is six. June is four.

Six. I do the math the way I imagine every wife in my position does the math. Six years ago, I was in a hospital bed after the third miscarriage, and Daniel was holding my hand and telling me it didn't matter, that we were already a family, the two of us.

And all the while he had a family. He just didn't have it with me.

"I want you to leave," I say.

"Claire. It's almost midnight."

"Then you'd better hurry. You know the way, apparently."

He tries a few more times. He says he was going to tell me. He says it got away from him, one lie stacked on another until he couldn't see over the top. He says he loves me—he actually says it, standing in our kitchen in one sock—and I laugh so hard and so suddenly that he takes a step back from me.

In the end he goes upstairs and puts on jeans. I hear drawers opening and closing. He comes back down with an overnight bag—not the gym bag, he leaves the gym bag right where it is—and he stops at the back door with his hand on the knob.

"Can I have it back?" he asks. "The phone."

"No."

He looks at me for a long moment. I think he might argue. Then he nods, once, the way a man nods when he knows he's lost the right to argue about anything, and he goes out into the dark. I listen to his car back down the driveway. I listen until I can't hear it anymore.

The house is very quiet.

I fold the towels. I can't explain why, except that they're there, and they're still a little warm, and my mother always said you can survive anything if you keep your hands busy. I fold them in thirds the way Daniel hates and I stack them on the dryer.

Then I put the kettle on and sit down at the kitchen table with the cheap black phone in front of me, and I look at those two little girls on the porch step for a long time.

Ellie and June.

Nora.

Tomorrow, people are going to ask me questions. My sister. Our friends. Probably a lawyer. They'll ask me how I found out, and I'll tell them about the laundry and the gym bag and the eleven forty-seven glowing on the microwave. They'll ask me how I'm holding up, and I'll tell them I can't stop shaking.

The kettle starts to whistle. I get up to take it off the burner, and as I reach for it I happen to look down at my hands.

They're perfectly still.

They were the whole time.

I pour the water. I sit back down. I turn the black phone face down on the table, the way Daniel does with his.
