Claire

Scroll

By morning, Daniel has called fourteen times.

Not on the black phone. That one stays quiet, face down on the kitchen table where I left it, like an animal playing dead. He calls my real phone, the one with our wedding photo as the lock screen, the one he bought me for our tenth anniversary and set up himself because he said I'd only mess up the settings.

Fourteen missed calls. Six voicemails. I don't listen to any of them.

Fourteen.

I call my sister at 7:02 a.m. Early enough to sound like I haven't slept. Late enough not to sound crazy.

Megan picks up on the second ring with a toddler shrieking in the background and the radio on. "Claire? What's wrong?"

That's the thing about calling someone at seven in the morning. They always know something is wrong before you say a word. You don't have to do any of the work.

"Daniel," I say, and then I stop, and I let the silence go on long enough that she turns the radio off.

I tell her everything. The gym bag. The phone. Ellie's tooth. The two little girls in yellow raincoats. I tell her about Hartford and the conference, and how he met her the same year I lost the second baby, and I hear my sister make a sound I have never heard her make before, a kind of low animal noise, like she's been kicked.

"Oh, Claire. Oh, honey. I'm getting in the car."

"No. Don't. You have the kids."

"I don't care about the kids."

"Megan." I make my voice small. "Please. I just need a day. I haven't slept at all. I'm going to try to lie down."

She doesn't like it. She makes me promise to call her tonight, and she makes me promise not to do anything stupid, and then she says the thing I knew she would say, the thing everyone is going to say over the next few weeks.

"I always thought he was too good to be true."

She didn't. Megan loves Daniel. Everyone loves Daniel. He remembers birthdays and he fixes things without being asked and he's the one at every party who notices you're standing alone and comes over with two drinks. But it doesn't matter what she thought before. What matters is what she'll remember thinking, and after today she'll remember that she always knew.

That's how people work. You just have to give them the ending, and they'll write the rest of the story backward for you.

There's no address on the black phone.

Daniel was careful about that. There are no emails, no maps, no saved locations. Just months of messages from N and a camera roll of the same gray porch, again and again: the girls on the steps, the girls in a plastic pool, a pumpkin on the railing with a crooked smile cut into it.

But in one picture, behind June's head, there's a porch post with brass numbers on it. 41. And in another, from last winter, there's a snowplow going by with the name of a town stenciled on its side.

Linden Falls. Forty minutes from here. I've driven through it a dozen times on my way to the outlet mall, past the gas station and the bakery and the white church with the clock that always says ten past three.

It takes me nine minutes online to find a gray house at 41 Orchard Lane. It's that easy. Seven years of lies, and it takes nine minutes to find where he kept them.

I shower. I put on jeans and Daniel's old college sweatshirt, the soft gray one with the hole in the cuff, because I want to look like a woman who grabbed whatever was on the floor. I don't put on makeup. I look in the mirror for a long time and decide I look exactly right.

Then I get in the car.

· · ·

I type the address into the GPS, and a cheerful woman's voice tells me to turn left out of the cul-de-sac.

I drive with both hands on the wheel. The radio is off. The maple trees on our street are just starting to turn, and I think about how Daniel always says the first red leaf of the year is a warning. I never asked him a warning of what. I wish I had.

On the highway, I pass a minivan full of kids watching a movie on a screen in the back seat, and I think about Ellie and June. I wonder if they have a minivan. I wonder if Daniel drives it. I wonder if he sings in the car with them the way he never sings in the car with me.

Three years ago, we drove through Linden Falls together. It was the Saturday after Thanksgiving, and I wanted a new winter coat, and Daniel offered to drive me to the outlets, which should have told me something right there, because Daniel hates the outlets more than he hates the dentist.

He took the back roads instead of the highway. He said it was prettier. And it was. The hills were bare and brown, and there was frost on the fields, and he held my hand across the console the whole way, which he hadn't done in a long time.

Then we came into Linden Falls, and he let go.

I remember it exactly. He took his hand back to put it on the wheel, and he sat up a little straighter, and he stopped talking. We passed the gas station. We passed the bakery. We stopped at the light by the white church, and he looked straight ahead at the red light the whole time it was red, like a man trying very hard not to look at something.

"You okay?" I asked him.

"Fine," he said. "Just tired."

The light turned green. He drove on, and two miles past the town line he reached over and took my hand again, and started talking about the coat I wanted, and whether I should get the black one or the camel.

I got the camel. I still have it. I wore it all that winter, and every time I put it on, I thought about the way he looked at that red light.

I have always been very good at remembering things.

After the second exit, I stop listening to the GPS. I don't really need it. The roads out here are simple, and I've always had a good sense of direction.

Linden Falls looks the way it always looks. The gas station. The bakery. The white church with its clock stuck at ten past three. I turn right at the church and then left onto Orchard Lane, a street of small, tidy houses with leaf piles at the curb and pumpkins on the steps.

Number 41 is the fourth house on the right.

41 Orchard Lane.

It's smaller than our house. Gray siding, white trim, a porch with two wicker chairs and a wind chime made of old spoons. There's a pot of orange mums on the top step, and by the front door, hanging from two little brass hooks at the height of a child's reach, two yellow raincoats.

I park across the street, two houses down, and I turn off the engine.

I don't know what I expected. A mansion, maybe. A villain's lair. Something that would explain it. But it's just a house. It's the kind of house you'd draw if someone asked you to draw a home.

I sit there for a long time. Long enough that the engine stops ticking. Long enough that a man walks past with a golden retriever and glances into my car and keeps walking.

Then the front door opens.

She comes out with a mug in her hand. She's younger than me, but not by as much as I'd hoped. Dark hair pulled back in a knot, a long cardigan the color of oatmeal, bare feet on the cold porch boards in October. She isn't beautiful, exactly. She's something more irritating than beautiful. She looks rested.

Nora.

She stands at the top of the steps and sips her coffee and looks out at the street the way you look at the weather. I slide down in my seat. It's a ridiculous thing to do, a cartoon thing, but my body does it before I can stop it.

Her eyes move along the street. The leaf piles. The man with the retriever, now at the corner. The parked cars.

My car.

She stops.

I tell myself she can't see me. The sun is on my windshield. I'm forty yards away, slumped in my seat in a gray sweatshirt. I'm just a parked car on a street full of parked cars.

Nora sets her mug down on the porch railing.

She walks down the steps in her bare feet, across her little lawn, and across the street. She doesn't hurry. She doesn't look both ways. She walks straight to my car the way you'd walk to your own mailbox, and she stops beside my window, and she bends down so her face is level with mine.

Then she knocks on the glass. Two light taps with one knuckle.

Tap. Tap.

I could start the car. I could drive away. I think about it. I actually put my hand on the key.

Instead, I press the button, and the window slides down between us, and the cold October air comes in smelling of leaves and her coffee.

Up close she has gray eyes. Not like Daniel's. Lighter. Paler. The color of a lake under ice.

She doesn't look surprised. She doesn't look afraid, or angry, or guilty. She looks at me the way a doctor looks at a test result she's been expecting.

"You must be Claire," she says.

I open my mouth. Nothing comes out. I had a speech. I wrote it in my head on the highway, word by word, and now I can't find the first word.

"He told me you'd never find out," Nora says. She tilts her head a little, studying my face. My unwashed hair. Daniel's sweatshirt with the hole in the cuff. "I told him you would."

Behind her, across the street, the wind moves the two yellow raincoats on their hooks. The spoon chime rings softly.

"The girls are at school until three," she says. "Would you like to come in? I just made coffee."

I've spent a very long time thinking about this day. I've thought about every word, every look, every way it could go.

I never once thought about this.

For the first time in a long time, I don't know what happens next.
